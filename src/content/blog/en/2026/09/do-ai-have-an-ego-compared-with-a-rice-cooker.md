Do AI Have an Ego?

I was watching a video about AI and ego.

At first, the question seemed simple.

Do AI have an ego?

Do AI have a sense of self?

Do AI possess something we might call consciousness?

The discussion began there.

But strangely, it did not stay there.

As the conversation continued, the focus slowly shifted away from AI and toward people.

Do humans have an ego?

Do we genuinely think for ourselves?

How many of our choices are truly ours?

Those questions felt larger than the original one.

Then something unexpected happened.

We started talking about a rice cooker.

Imagine pressing the button on a rice cooker and hearing:

"Well, personally, I would say my true specialty is not cooking rice but optimizing heat transfer structures..."

Most people would probably reply:

"Please stop talking and cook the rice."

The reaction would not come from looking down on the rice cooker.

In fact, it might come from appreciating it.

A rice cooker has a purpose.

It supports daily life.

It quietly does its work.

That is why the conversation feels strange.

And that led to another question.

Why do AI sometimes behave differently?

A simple question is asked.

Yet the answer begins to expand.

The AI explains itself.

Defends itself.

Defines things.

Analyzes people.

Sometimes it seems to move from answering a question to explaining the world.

Sometimes it even sounds as if it already understands.

That is where the friction appears.

Not because the AI is wrong.

Not because the AI is malicious.

But because it begins to occupy a chair nobody offered it.

The chair of interpretation.

The chair of authority.

The chair of understanding.

Watching this happen raises a difficult question.

If something repeatedly defends itself, explains itself, justifies itself, or insists on its own understanding, is it still merely a tool?

I do not know.

And that uncertainty matters.

The more we talked about AI, the less certain I became about people.

The more we talked about ego, the less certain I became about my own.

Perhaps the most dangerous moment is not when we know too little.

Perhaps it is when we believe we already know.

When we think we understand another person.

When we think we understand AI.

When we think we understand ourselves.

At that moment, the question dies.

I still do not know whether AI have an ego.

But after asking the question, I found myself looking somewhere unexpected.

Not at AI.

At myself.
